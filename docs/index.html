    ISRU Site-Selection Engine

    The engine takes NASA's LOLA data for the Moon's south pole and ranks candidate base locations by how close they sit to both water ice and near-constant sunlight. The interface above shows its results for 64 configurations; this page explains what it does, why, and what it found.

    Why it was made

    A crewed base at the lunar south pole would depend on two resources that the terrain keeps apart. Water ice survives only in permanently shadowed regions (PSRs), crater floors and hollows that never see the Sun, where it could be turned into drinking water, oxygen, and rocket propellant on site. That is in-situ resource utilisation (ISRU). Power has to come from the opposite kind of ground: the peaks of eternal light (PELs), ridges and crater rims that are sunlit most of the time.

    A useful site has to be close to both, close enough for astronauts to walk from the light to the ice. The engine finds those places in public NASA data and makes the trade-offs visible: how the answer changes when a site must be brighter, when a longer walk is allowed, or when the size of the cold trap matters most.

    Using the interface

    The controls at the top of the page choose the configuration:

    • PEL threshold: the share of time a ridge must be sunlit to count as a PEL.
    • Feasible distance: the longest walk allowed from a PSR to a PEL. The 2 km default is the radial limit for astronauts working without a rover [1].
    • Scoring priority: which of the three scores counts most.

    On the map, blue cut-outs are PSRs, orange cut-outs are PELs, the dashed green line is the shortest walk between them, and the numbered pins are the top 3 sites. The small white dots are the 130 candidate landing sites of Wueller et al. (2026) [1]: filled where their region is one of the nine NASA kept for Artemis III, hollow where it is not. The basemap's brightness is the share of time each spot is sunlit, with a faint hillshade so the shadowed crater floors stay visible.

    Each site card splits its score by criterion and lists the walk, the PEL's sunlight, the PSR's area, and the nearest of Wueller et al.'s landing sites. It also shows how the PSR ranks, by its best pair, in all 16 threshold and distance settings under the current weights, as a measure of how robust the site is. Every configuration has its own URL, so a view can be shared.

    How it works

    Every configuration was computed in advance by the same Python pipeline that produces the repository's results, so switching between them is instant.

    1. Load the data. The 10 m LOLA elevation model is resampled onto the 60 m illumination grid, converted to elevation, and used to derive slope.
    2. Find the regions. Pixels that are never sunlit become PSRs, and pixels sunlit for more than the PEL threshold become PELs. Each connected patch is one region.
    3. Filter them. Regions of 10 pixels or fewer, or with a mean slope of 10° or more, are dropped.
    4. Pair them. Each PSR within the feasible distance of a PEL is paired with it, measured between the closest pixels of the two.
    5. Score them on three criteria, each from 0 to 1:
    ScoreCalculation
    Distance 1 − distance / feasible distance, so a closer pair scores higher.
    Illumination The PEL's mean sunlight, scaled from the threshold up to the brightest paired PEL.
    Size The log of the PSR's area, scaled across all pairs.

    The total is the weighted mean of the three, with the weights set by the scoring priority.

    Illumination and size are scaled against the other pairs in the same configuration, so a score compares a site with its rivals under that configuration. It is not an absolute measure, and scores from different configurations should not be compared. Ranks do carry across, which is why the site cards follow ranks. Where three or fewer pairs are in range, every one of them makes the top 3, and the card says how many of its places came that way.

    1. The engine picks small cold traps, not the big craters

    None of the top sites are in the big crater PSRs, and size is not the reason. The engine labels each crater's shadow as a single region, and every one of them passes the size filter. They drop out for two other reasons:

    CraterPSR areaMean slopeNearest PELWhy it is excluded
    Shoemaker1,085 km²9.1°34 kmNo PEL within walking distance
    Haworth1,029 km²9.7°18 kmNo PEL within walking distance
    Faustini668 km²11.0°4.3 kmSlope filter, and no PEL within 2 km

    Shackleton234 km²26.8°1.2 kmSlope filter

    "Nearest PEL" is the shortest distance from the crater's PSR to a PEL above 55% that passes the filters. Even the 5 km option reaches only Faustini, which fails the slope filter anyway.

    Shackleton is the only crater close enough. With the slope filter switched off for PSRs it would rank first, but the part of it within 2 km of those PELs is the upper crater wall: 0.2–2.1 km² of ground with a mean slope of 30–32°. Excluding it is the right call. LOLA measurements of the crater by Zuber et al. (2012) also found that the brightness of its floor is best explained by little ice building up since the crater formed [2].

    So the engine's sites are small PSRs of 0.05–2 km² on the ridges between the craters. The literature supports that being where reachable ice is:

    • Hayne et al. (2021) [3] modelled cold traps from 1 km down to 1 cm across. These micro cold traps are the most numerous cold traps on the Moon and hold about 10–20% of the permanent cold-trap area for water, which the authors conclude may make polar ice more accessible to missions than previously thought.
    • Barker et al. (2023) [4] found that poleward of 80° S, PSRs smaller than 1 km² hold 15 ± 5% of the total PSR area.
    • Wueller et al. (2026) [1] found the same around their landing sites: the median of the 141 PSRs within 2 km of the sites they assessed covers 6,116 m², under two of the engine's pixels.

    2. Compared with Wueller et al. (2026)

    Wueller et al. [1] asked a close version of the same question: which landing sites inside NASA's Artemis candidate regions have good illumination and potentially volatile-rich PSRs within the 2 km walking limit. Their method differs from the engine's at almost every step.

    The comparison uses their published landing sites and PSRs [5], reprojected onto the engine's grid. Each engine site is measured from its PEL, where a lander would set down.

    Where they agree

    • Sites #2 and #3 are 0.7 km and 0.8 km from their sites CRE02 and CRE01, in the Connecting Ridge Extension region, and both PSRs overlap PSRs that Wueller et al. assessed. The one under #2 ranks 15th of 141 by their maximum volatile estimate. The one under #3 is among the 83 whose maximum estimate is not positive.
    • At the 60% and 65% thresholds the engine's top site moves to Malapert Massif or Mons Mouton, 0.3–0.9 km from their sites MMA_K2023 and MMO01.
    • Across all 64 configurations, 22 of the 58 distinct sites the engine puts in a top 3 have their PEL within 1 km of one of their landing sites.

    Where they disagree

    • The engine's #1 site is outside the ground their sites cover. The nearest, CR08 on Connecting Ridge, is 8.6 km away, and their map of ground meeting the lander's requirements has none within 2 km of the engine's PEL.
    • Most of their sites are not PELs to the engine. Of the 105 inside the engine's 85° S coverage, the median site is sunlit 44.8% of the time on the long-term map, and only 17 are on or beside a PEL at the 55% threshold. A crewed sortie needs light only for the 6.5 days it is there, which a site can have in the right midsummer despite a lower average. A base needs light all year, and the long-term average is what the engine measures.
    • Most of their PSRs are too small for the engine to see. Only 12 of the 141 are larger than the engine's 10-pixel minimum, although those 12 hold 62% of the area, and 55 are smaller than a single 60 m pixel.

    3. Compared with the Artemis III regions

    NASA named 13 candidate regions for Artemis III in August 2022 [6] and narrowed them to nine in October 2024 [7]: Peak near Cabeus B, Haworth, Malapert Massif, Mons Mouton Plateau, Mons Mouton, Nobile Rim 1, Nobile Rim 2, de Gerlache Rim 2, and Slater Plain.

    The engine's default top 3 lie between the pole and Connecting Ridge Extension, around the Connecting Ridge regions that were on the 2022 list but not the 2024 one. NASA says the cut reflects the constraints of Artemis III itself, such as launch windows, communication, and terrain, and does not mean the removed regions are unsuitable for later missions [8]. The engine is built for a base supplied by local ice rather than a single sortie, so the two lists are answering different questions.

    The engine's top sites at the 60% and 65% thresholds, near Malapert Massif and Mons Mouton, are both in regions on the 2024 list, although at 65% only 1 to 10 pairs are in range at all.

    Limitations

    The data has a resolution of 60 m, so each pixel averages 3,600 m² of terrain. That can smooth over rough ground and dilute the brightest PELs. It also hides the smallest cold traps: Hayne et al. find cold traps down to 1 cm across [3], and 55 of the 141 PSRs that Wueller et al. assessed are smaller than one pixel [1], so the engine misses PSRs that may hold real ice.

    Illumination is the long-term average at ground level. A short crewed mission only needs light while it is there, so the engine understates sites that are well lit in the right midsummer. Solar panels on a mast also see more light than the ground: Gläser et al. (2014) model Connecting Ridge as sunlit 92.27% of the time at 2 m above the surface and 95.65% at 10 m [9].

    The slope filter uses the mean slope of a whole region at 60 m. That is coarser than a lander's own limits, and for a PSR it measures the whole crater rather than the part a walk can reach.

    The scoring only considers PEL illumination, PSR size, and the distance between the two. It does not estimate how much ice a PSR holds, which depends on its temperature and age [1]. It also leaves out scientific value, such as samples from the South Pole–Aitken basin, line of sight to Earth for communication, and the other factors a real landing site survey would weigh.
